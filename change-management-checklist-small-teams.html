Change Management Checklist for Small Teams: The 15-Minute Gate That Stops Friday Night Outages

The four-question gate, the change log that takes two minutes, the rollback plan that takes five, and the freeze windows that protect your weekends.

Change management in a small team is not a CAB (change advisory board), not a ticketing system, and not a 20-page process document. It's a 15-minute conversation before you touch production that asks four questions, writes down the answers, and has a rollback plan. That's it. The teams that do this have Friday nights free. The teams that don't have a Slack channel called #incidents that lights up at 6 PM because someone "just a small config change"d the load balancer.

The four-question gate

Before any change to production — code deploy, config update, DNS change, firewall rule, even a "quick" database migration — answer these four questions out loud (or in writing if you're solo):

  1. What exactly is changing, and what does it touch? Not "updating the app" — "deploying v2.3.1, changes the auth middleware, touches the user session table." If you can't name the blast radius, you can't assess the risk. The release checklist has the full pre-deploy verification; the gate is the summary.
  2. What's the rollback plan, and have we tested it? "Revert the deploy" is not a rollback plan if the migration already ran. A rollback plan names the exact steps, the time they take, and who runs them. If the rollback takes longer than the change window, you don't have a rollback plan — you have hope. The post-deploy verification checklist tells you when to trigger it.
  3. Who needs to know, and have we told them? If customers will see a 30-second blip, the status page needs a scheduled message. If the support team will get tickets, they need a heads-up. If the CEO's demo is in 20 minutes, it's not the right window.
  4. Is this the right time? Friday at 4 PM is never the right time for a non-emergency change. The last day of the month is never the right time if you run payroll. Define your freeze windows once and stop arguing about them every week.

The change log (2 minutes, not 2 hours)

You need exactly five fields: when (date/time), who (person making the change), what (one-line description), rollback (yes/no, and how), result (success/failed/rolled back). A shared Google Sheet, a Slack channel, a whiteboard — the format doesn't matter. The discipline of writing it down does. When something breaks on Wednesday and nobody remembers what changed on Tuesday, the change log is how you find it. The weekly ops review should scan the change log every week — patterns emerge (the same person's changes fail, the same service breaks after deploys) that you can't see in real time.

Freeze windows

Define them once, publish them, and enforce them without exception. Common freeze windows for small teams:

  • Friday after 2 PM — nothing to production. The weekend is not a testing environment.
  • Last 3 business days of the month — if you run payroll, billing, or month-end reporting. These are the highest-stakes days; a change that breaks invoicing costs more than a week of delay.
  • During known peak events — Black Friday, tax deadline week, conference week. The seasonal peak readiness checklist identifies these in advance.
  • When on-call is understaffed — if the person who can fix it is on leave, it's not the week to change it.

Emergency changes bypass the freeze — but they still get the four-question gate, and they get logged with "EMERGENCY" in the what field so the weekly review can ask why.

What kills change management in small teams

  • "We're too small for process." You're too small for a CAB. You're not too small for four questions and a spreadsheet. The process that fits a 5-person team is 15 minutes. The outage that comes from skipping it is 4 hours.
  • The rollback plan is "we'll figure it out." The time to figure out the rollback is before the change, not after. If you can't write a rollback plan in 5 minutes, the change is too complex to do without breaking it into smaller steps. The runaway automation runbook covers the specific case of automated changes that go wrong.
  • Nobody reviews the change log. A change log that nobody reads is a diary, not a tool. The weekly review checklist includes a change-log scan — 5 minutes, every week, look for patterns.
  • The freeze window has exceptions every week. If everything is an emergency, nothing is. Track the exceptions. If one person accounts for 80% of emergency changes, that's a process problem, not a change problem.

Worked example: the e-commerce team that stopped breaking Fridays

A 6-person e-commerce team had a pattern: deploy on Friday afternoon, something breaks at 7 PM, the on-call engineer spends Saturday morning fixing it. The change log showed that 4 of the last 6 Saturday incidents traced to Friday deploys. The fix was not "stop deploying" — it was "stop deploying on Friday after 2 PM." They moved the deploy window to Wednesday 10 AM, added the four-question gate (which took 12 minutes the first time, 6 minutes by the third), and kept the change log in a Slack channel (#change-log) that the whole team could see. Saturday incidents dropped to zero in the first month. The Wednesday deploys still occasionally broke things — but they broke at 10 AM on a Wednesday when the whole team was online, not at 7 PM on a Friday when one person was on call.
