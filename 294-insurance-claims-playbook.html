What This Is

Insurance is an expense that pays for itself when you need it. The problem is that most businesses don't file claims often enough to know how to file them correctly. This playbook gives you a systematic process: document incidents, file claims quickly, negotiate payouts, and avoid future gaps in coverage.

Why You Need This

  • Speed matters: Most policies pay out faster if you file within 24-48 hours.
  • Details matter: The more evidence you provide, the higher your payout.
  • Documentation saves money: You'll remember what happened if you write it down.
  • Dispute resolution: If the insurer rejects your claim, you'll know how to appeal.

Step 1: Claim Readiness Checklist (15 Minutes)

Before an incident happens, do this checklist. It takes 15 minutes but saves you weeks of stress and lost revenue.

Claim Readiness Checklist

  • [ ] Policy number and renewal date: Save this in 2 locations (vault + cloud)
  • [ ] Policy coverage summary: Know what you're insured for (property, liability, business interruption)
  • [ ] Named insured contact: Who signs the claim?
  • [ ] Authorized representatives: Who can speak to the insurer on your behalf?
  • [ ] Emergency contact for insurer: Who to call first?
  • [ ] Evidence storage: Where do you store photos, videos, and documents?
  • [ ] Claim timeline tracking: Spreadsheet or simple log to track dates and updates
  • [ ] Business interruption calculator: How much revenue are you losing per day?
  • [ ] Vendor contracts: Do you have backup vendor contracts (from the supply chain playbook)?
  • [ ] Loss assessment: Who will appraise your property/loss?
  • [ ] Legal representation: Do you have a lawyer on retainer for disputes?
  • [ ] State insurance regulator contact: If you need to escalate

Step 2: Immediate Response to Incident (1 Hour)

When an incident happens, speed is your best friend. Follow these steps in order.

Incident Response Timeline

0-30 minutes: Secure the scene, document the incident, notify stakeholders 30-60 minutes: Document everything, contact your insurance broker if you have one, start the claim process

Documentation Checklist (Do This First)

  • Photos: Take photos and videos from multiple angles. Note the date/time in the file name.
  • Witness statements: Get names, contact info, and statements from anyone who saw what happened.
  • Written account: Write a narrative: What, when, where, who, how, why. Include timelines.
  • Financial impact: Calculate immediate losses (property damage, lost equipment, emergency expenses).
  • Business interruption: Calculate ongoing losses (revenue per day, projected duration).
  • Revenue proof: Bank statements, invoices, receipts showing revenue before and after the incident.
Pro Tip: Create a permanent "incident folder" on your server. Every claim gets its own subfolder with photos, documents, and correspondence.

Step 3: File the Claim (30 Minutes)

Don't wait. Most policies require claims to be filed within 24-48 hours of an incident.

Claim Filing Checklist

  • Call the insurer: Get the claim number immediately
  • Submit in writing: Email your broker/agent + insurer with all documentation
  • Attach evidence: Photos, videos, receipts, witness statements
  • Send summary: Short narrative + estimated financial impact
  • Request timeline: Ask for their expected claim processing timeline
  • Confirm receipt: Get written confirmation from the insurer

Step 4: Negotiate the Payout (Ongoing)

Insurance companies often start low. They're businesses too, and they want to minimize payouts. You negotiate based on evidence and documentation.

Payout Negotiation Strategy

  • Be specific: Don't say "$50,000." Say "$48,200 for property damage + $1,800 for business interruption."
  • Attach receipts: Every dollar must be backed by proof. Make it easy for them to pay.
  • Cite policy language: Reference specific policy clauses that cover your claim.
  • Provide projections: Show them your business interruption calculator. "We lost $X/day for Y days."
  • Be professional: Don't get emotional. Stay factual and objective.
  • Document everything: Every conversation, every email, every phone call.
Negotiation Email Template:

Subject: Re: Claim #12345 - [Type of Claim]

Dear [Insurance Adjuster Name],

Thank you for your update on claim #12345. Based on my review of the policy terms and the documentation provided, I respectfully request a payout of [Specific Amount].

Policy Coverage: [Cite specific clause that covers this claim]

Calculated Damages:
1. Property damage: $[Amount] (receipts attached)
2. Business interruption: $[Amount]/day × [Number] days = $[Total]
3. Emergency expenses: $[Amount] (receipts attached)

Total Claim Amount: $[Specific Amount]

I have attached all supporting documentation, including photos, receipts, revenue statements, and witness statements. If you need additional information, please let me know and I will provide it within 24 hours.

I look forward to your response.

Best regards,
[Your Name]
[Your Title]
[Contact Information]

Step 5: Handle Disputes and Appeals (If Needed)

If the insurer denies your claim or offers less than you expect, don't get angry. They're not personal. They're a business decision. You'll either get the money or you won't. Either way, you'll know what happened.

Appeal Timeline

Day 1: Review the denial letter. Understand why they rejected the claim.
Day 3: Submit a written appeal with additional evidence.
Day 10: Insurer responds. If rejected, request a review by a senior adjuster.
Day 30: Escalate to state insurance regulator if necessary.
Appeal Letter Template:

Subject: Appeal - Claim #12345 - [Type of Claim]

Dear [Insurance Adjuster Name],

I am writing to formally appeal the decision to deny/underpay claim #12345. I respectfully request that the claim be reviewed.

Basis for Appeal:
1. [Evidence 1]: [Explain why this supports your claim]
2. [Evidence 2]: [Explain why this supports your claim]
3. [Evidence 3]: [Explain why this supports your claim]

I have attached additional documentation that was not included in the original claim. [Briefly describe the new evidence]

I am prepared to provide any additional information needed to support my claim.

Thank you for your consideration.

Best regards,
[Your Name]
[Your Title]
[Contact Information]

Step 6: Learn and Prevent (Ongoing)

Every claim is a data point. Learn what caused the incident and prevent it from happening again.

Post-Claim Review Template

  • Root cause: What actually caused the incident?
  • Prevention: What will you do differently next time?
  • Policy gaps: Was something not covered that should have been?
  • Process improvements: What part of the claims process was slow or difficult?
  • Documentation improvements: What evidence would have strengthened the claim?
  • Commercial solutions: Is there insurance that would cover this better next time?

Step 7: Reassess Coverage (Quarterly)

Your business changes. Your coverage needs to change with it.

Coverage Review Questions

  • Have you grown? Your liability limit might be too low for your new size.
  • Have you changed products/services? New risk = new coverage.
  • Have you added equipment? Make sure it's covered for replacement cost.
  • Have you moved? Different hazards in different locations.
  • Have you hired more employees? Workers' compensation and liability risks increase.
  • Have you expanded internationally? Now you need global coverage.

Worked Example

The $120,000 Success Story

Your warehouse flooded. Water destroyed 80% of your inventory. Your property insurance covered the building. You also had business interruption insurance. Here's how you handled it:

  • Day 1: You called your broker and insurer within 30 minutes. They assigned an adjuster within 4 hours.
  • Day 2: You took photos and videos from every angle. You documented every piece of destroyed equipment with receipts.
  • Day 3: You calculated your business interruption loss: $15,000/day × 30 days = $450,000.
  • Day 4: You submitted your claim with all documentation.
  • Month 2: The insurer offered $320,000. You rejected it, attached your revenue statements and 30-day revenue projection, and cited the business interruption clause. They accepted.

Result: You recovered 71% of your projected loss. You didn't lose a year of revenue. Your workflow worked.
