The Shift Handover Log: The Ten-Minute Sheet That Stops the Next Shift Paying for This One’s Surprises

A shift handover log is not paperwork; it is a firewall. Every shift ends with knowledge only the outgoing crew holds — the walk-in that read warm at lunch, the dishwasher that’s making the new noise, the porter who didn’t show, the supplier who called about Monday’s delivery. When that knowledge leaves in people’s heads instead of on a sheet, the next shift rediscovers it the hard way, hours later, at full price. Five minutes at the crossover, seven lines, and the day’s surprises get handed over before they become the next shift’s emergencies. The equipment downtime log records what broke and what it cost; the handover log is what stops the breaking from happening in the first place.

1. Why the crossover — the five-minute arithmetic

Problems do not respect shift boundaries, but they do follow the cheapest path: the cheapest person to tell is whoever just noticed it, and the cheapest time to act on a problem is while the person who understands it is still standing on the floor. A walk-in that flagged at lunch is a thirty-second recheck at handover; the same walk-in discovered by the dinner crew at 6 p.m. is a stock decision, a work order and a bad night. The log moves every open issue across the boundary while it is still cheap — and it does it in writing, because “I told the manager” is the most expensive sentence in operations. It is the same reason the work order exists: verbal problems stay verbal until they cost money.

2. The log itself — seven lines per handover

One sheet at the crossover point (the office desk, not a shared drive nobody opens), dated and initialed by both shifts, seven lines:

  1. Date, shift, and both initials. The outgoing shift signs for what it leaves; the incoming shift signs for what it accepts. Both names in the same row is what turns a note into a contract.
  2. Open work orders. Every ticket from the work order queue that is still open, with its number and current state — so a ticket opened at 11 a.m. doesn’t quietly die with the crew that opened it.
  3. Equipment watching. Anything behaving oddly but not yet broken — “walk-in #2 air read 43°F at lunch, food OK, recheck pending” — the front line of the temperature log workflow.
  4. People. Absences, late starts, no-shows, and the one person on the floor who covers a gap. This is the line the coverage plan reads the next morning.
  5. Suppliers and deliveries. The phone call about Monday’s order, the short delivery accepted at the door, the invoice dispute opened — things that decay badly overnight.
  6. Money. The till variance, the refund issued, the invoice flagged. Unexplained variances age like milk.
  7. The one thing the next shift must know. The single most important line, written even when it is “nothing — good shift.” An empty “must know” line means someone stopped thinking, and the log’s value dies the day it becomes a rubber stamp.

3. The walk-the-floor rule

The handover is not a document exchange; it is a five-minute walk. The outgoing and arriving leads stand at the machine, not at the desk: recheck the flagged walk-in together, listen to the odd dishwasher noise together, open the chiller together. Two people at the problem cuts the mis-transfer rate to near zero — the incoming shift hears it, sees it and owns it in the same minute. A handover note that says “something weird with the ice machine” transfers nothing; a walk that ends with “the pump cycles every ninety seconds, work order #0144 is open” transfers everything.

4. The no-surprises clause

One rule, said out loud at the crossover: nothing discovered after the handover is blamed on the incoming shift. In exchange, anything the outgoing shift knew and did not write down is theirs to come back for. The clause isn’t about punishment; it’s about incentives — it makes writing things down the cheap option and withholding them the expensive one. Teams without the clause develop the worst handover habit of all: the deliberate silence, where a bad afternoon gets quietly handed to the night crew like a hot pan without a handle.

5. The morning answer — 30 seconds

The arriving manager opens the sheet and answers three questions in thirty seconds: What is broken or pending? What is today’s risk? What did yesterday’s shift already handle, so I don’t redo it? Every line with a name and a number is actionable; every line without one is a story. The log also feeds the monthly ops report — a month of handover lines is a month of near-misses, absences and equipment drift in one stack, the raw material the trends are made of.

6. The five traps

  • The notebook in the drawer. A handover that lives anywhere but the crossover point gets skipped exactly on the chaotic days it matters most.
  • Verbal-only handover. “I told her” is not a transfer; it is a rumor with a time stamp.
  • The rubber stamp. Seven “all good” lines in a row means nobody is thinking. A blank “must know” line is the canary.
  • The blame-transfer sheet. When the log becomes a list of accusations, the next shift stops writing honest lines and starts writing defensive ones.
  • No follow-through. Every open item hands forward until someone closes it — a line carried across three handovers with no name attached is a work order in disguise; give it one.

7. Worked example — the 40-seat restaurant’s 4 p.m.

Lunch service on the Tuesday: walk-in #2 read air 43°F at 11:40 (temperature log flagged, recheck pending). The lunch lead writes the handover’s equipment line before leaving — “walk-in #2 flagged 43°F/41°F, recheck at 16:15, work order #0143 open, gasket on order.” The dinner lead walks to the walk-in at the crossover, rechecks, finds it stable at 41°F, and signs on. Total transfer cost: four minutes. The alternative — the flag left in a lunch crew’s memory that clocks out at 4 — is the same walk-in discovered during Friday’s $7,300 stock quarter, which is exactly the outcome the previous quarter’s downtime log shows it costs. The handover log is the cheapest link in that whole chain.
