Weather-day closures — decide the night before, not in the parking lot

A weather day is the only closure you get to schedule in advance. The storm is on a forecast; the decision doesn't have to be made in the dark at 6am by whichever employee lives closest. A weather day decided at 6pm the night before is a planned event: notice goes out while customers are still awake, staff sleep in instead of driving on ice to a dark building, and the reopen is a known date. The same storm decided at 6am is a scramble: a voicemail recorded in a panic, a website banner nobody remembers to post, and one employee whose commute became the story of the week. This page gives you the T-14h decision window, the trigger board, what closes versus what still runs, the channel sequence, and the five traps — so the weather calls the day, not your morning.

1. The T-14h decision window

  1. Call it by 6pm the evening before for a next-day closure. That's roughly fourteen hours of notice: enough for every channel, every booked customer, and every staff schedule. Later than that, you're making a 6am decision with 6am information quality and 6am panic.
  2. One named weather decider. Not a committee text thread. The decider is the person who owns the calendar; they make the call, everyone else executes it. Write the name on this page and on the business continuity plan.
  3. Two-source rule. The decider looks at two independent sources (a national forecast service and the local emergency-management or school-district call) plus the road reality outside their own window. One source is a rumor; two agreeing sources is a forecast.
  4. A provisional decision is allowed. If the forecast is genuinely on the fence at 6pm, call a delayed open instead of staying silent: "opening at 11am instead of 7am — update by 8:30am." A provisional call that updates is ten times better than a normal-looking morning that isn't. The full wording, the update-deadline rule, and the never-delay-twice flip are in the delayed opening notice template.
  5. The 6pm call is communicated like any other closure — same seven-channel sequence as the temporary closure notice, starting tonight, not at dawn.

2. The trigger board (write the thresholds down before winter)

Decide in advance what the forecast has to say for the closure to fire. The point is to remove judgment from the moment of maximum fatigue. A small-business trigger board looks like:

  • Ice or freezing rain forecast on the morning commute — close. Ice is the one weather event with no "we'll manage" middle ground; the morning commute is the dangerous part, not the day.
  • Snowfall above your road threshold (for most service businesses: the amount that means side streets aren't plowed by opening) — close, or delay to the afternoon.
  • School district closes — close, if your customers or staff are parents. The district decision is a free, local, already-made call about the same streets you share.
  • Emergency declaration or travel advisory in your county — close, no debate; this is also the line your insurer and licensor will ask about.
  • Extreme heat or cold warnings — close or shorten hours if the building can't hold safe temperatures; HVAC failure on a weather day is its own incident.

Paste the board into the continuity plan. If the thresholds live only in the owner's head, the night the owner has the flu is the night the thresholds change.

3. What closes and what still runs

A weather day closes the room, not necessarily the business. Write both lists before the first storm:

  • Closed: walk-in service, in-person appointments, deliveries, and any staff travel that isn't essential. Say it plainly — "the shop is closed Tuesday" — not "we are operating at reduced capacity" (nobody knows what that means).
  • Still runs: online orders that ship from a warehouse, gift cards, email and DMs, phone lines forwarded to the owner's cell, and any remote-only work your staff can do from home if the power holds. Every one of these is revenue that survives the storm only because someone wrote down that it should.
  • Rescheduled, not cancelled: every booked appointment gets a new date offered in the closure message itself. A weather day handled well is a rescheduling exercise, not a refund queue — see the cancellation-and-deposit policy question in the upfront deposit checklist.

4. The seven-channel sequence (same order every time)

  1. Door sign — printed at 6pm, on the door by close of business tonight. The sign says the closed day, the planned reopen, and nothing else.
  2. Website banner — the same two lines, at the top of every page, dated.
  3. Google Business Profile hours — set the closed day explicitly. A closed business whose Maps listing says OPEN on a snowed-in morning is the weather-day twin of the locked-door-OPEN-pin problem, and it's the channel customers check from the couch.
  4. Voicemail — record it tonight, calmly, in two sentences, with the reopen date. A voicemail recorded at 6am on the day is rushed, wrong, or silent.
  5. Email autoresponder — updated with the same two lines and the date the inbox returns to normal.
  6. Social (one post, all platforms) — the same text, one emoji of restraint, posted the evening before. No hourly "still closed!" updates; the single post with the reopen date is the update.
  7. Booked customers, directly — every person with an appointment for the closed day gets an individual message with two choices: a specific new time, or a credit. A blast post is not a notification to the person who arranged childcare and drove anyway because their appointment "didn't get cancelled."

The five weather-day traps

  • The 6am decision. The forecast said ice at midnight; the call is made at 5:55am by a staff member white-knuckling it in, and by 7am you've cancelled a morning's worth of booked appointments with a message that reaches the customers after they've arrived. The 6pm decision exists to make this impossible.
  • Weather-day-as-holiday optimism. "We'll just decide in the morning, it might be nice!" The cost of a wrong close is one quiet day. The cost of a wrong open is the employee in the ditch, the customer who fell in your parking lot, and the trust bill. When in doubt on ice: close, or delay to midday.
  • The reopen-while-iced lot. Reopening the business while the parking lot and walkway are still an ice rink moves the hazard from the road to your property — and onto your liability. The reopen gate includes: lot plowed, walk salted, and one person physically present to confirm before the sign flips. The verified-reopen gate applies to weather days more than any other closure.
  • Updating only one channel. The website banner posted, the voicemail forgotten, and the customer who calls because that's what the closure message told them to do — hears yesterday's recording saying you're open. Channel completeness is a checklist item, not a hope; tick all seven every time.
  • The silent staff thread. The closure is announced to customers but the staff group chat never gets the same clarity: who needs to come in at all (nobody), who's on for the reopen (who opens, who's backup), and what the pay policy is for the closed day (whatever your policy is, it was decided before the storm, in writing). Staff learn about their work day from your customer-facing post is the fastest morale leak a small business can make.

Worked example: a 22-seat diner and the Tuesday ice storm

A 22-seat diner, family-run, open 7am–2pm. Monday 5:40pm: the weather decider (the owner) checks two sources — freezing rain 4am–9am, county travel advisory "unnecessary travel discouraged until midday." 5:50pm the decision fires on the trigger board (ice + advisory): closed Tuesday morning, provisional 11am open. By 6:45pm the same evening: door sign printed and set, website banner up, Google hours flipped to closed-then-11am, voicemail re-recorded ("closed Tuesday morning, planning an 11am open — update by 8:30am either way"), autoresponder on, one social post, and all nine of Tuesday's morning reservations individually messaged: "Tuesday 7am booking moved to Wednesday 7am — reply with any time that suits, or take a credit." Total elapsed: 58 minutes, done in pajamas. Tuesday 8:30am: roads still glazed, so the 11am becomes "closed Tuesday entirely, open 7am Wednesday" — one paragraph, all seven channels, ten minutes. Wednesday 6:15am: lot plowed, walk salted, the owner stands in the doorway before the sign comes down. Nineteen of the twenty-two moved bookings show; two take credits; one no-show. The only cost of the storm was a quiet Tuesday and the salt.

Metrics (for the weather-day process itself)

  • 100% of closures decided by 6pm the evening before (or a delayed-open announced at 6pm instead).
  • All seven channels ticked within 90 minutes of the decision — every time, including the two everyone forgets (Google hours, voicemail).
  • Every booked customer contacted individually with a specific alternative, not a blast post.
  • Reopen confirmed by a person standing in the building, lot and walkway clear.
  • One page, kept with the continuity plan, holding the trigger board, the decider's name, and the staff pay line.
