Voicemail Greeting Script: The Ten-Minute Recording That Stops the Beep Losing the Customer

The quick version: the voicemail greeting is the second-most-used page of your business and the one nobody ever wrote on purpose. Six out of ten calls to small businesses that don't connect end at the beep — and whether the caller stays for the beep or hits redial on the next listing is decided by about four seconds of recorded voice. This page gives you the script that keeps them: five lines, four variations, the recording craft that makes a cheap microphone sound trustworthy, and the traps that quietly turn a mailbox into a leak.

Why callers hang up on the beep

A caller who reaches a voicemail is doing maths. They are estimating three things: will anyone hear this? how long until they call back? is there a faster way? A generic greeting — the carrier's default, or "you've reached [number], leave a message after the tone" — answers none of the three, so the estimate comes back unfavourable and the caller moves on to the next result in the search list. The hang-up is not rudeness; it is triage.

The fix is not charm. It is information, in this order: who you reached, when someone will actually listen, what to say now, and the path that doesn't involve a callback at all. Ten seconds of recording can answer all four. Most greetings answer zero.

The five-line script (ten minutes to record, once)

  1. Line 1 — the name and the promise. "You've reached Northside Plumbing." Not "you've reached the voicemail of" — the caller already knows whose phone it is; say the business name like you're glad they called.
  2. Line 2 — the back time. "We're with customers right now — we check this box every hour, and someone will call you back before 2 pm today." The specific time is the line that does the work. "As soon as possible" is an estimate; "before 2 pm" is a promise, and promises get callbacks.
  3. Line 3 — what to leave. "Leave your name, number, and the one thing you need done — a slow drain, no hot water, a leak — and we'll call you straight back with a time." Naming the three things callers actually need turns their rambling message into a workable ticket.
  4. Line 4 — the faster path. "If it's easier, text this number the same three things, or book a time at northside.com/book — both go straight to the same phone." The text path and the booking link are the two exits that don't require you to be near the phone, and they convert far better than a callback.
  5. Line 5 — the urgent path. "If water's going somewhere it shouldn't, press zero now for the on-call plumber." An escape hatch — a real option for the caller who cannot wait — is the difference between "they'll get back to me" and "they're dealing with me now."

Read it at half the speed that feels natural, then again at three-quarters. Every line that doesn't carry one of the five jobs comes out. The finished recording should be under twenty seconds; the beep arrives, the caller knows exactly what to do, and the maths they were doing comes back in your favour.

The four variations every business needs

One greeting is a leak on three days out of four. The mailbox needs four recordings, swapped in when the situation changes — most phone systems keep them on speed dial, and the swap takes ten seconds.

  • The workday greeting (default): the five lines above, tuned to today's backlog — "we check the box every hour" if the front desk is staffed, "before 2 pm" if it isn't.
  • The closed greeting: states the hours and the reopen time in the first sentence ("closed — back 7 am tomorrow"), then the text path with a booking link, then the urgent path. This is the one that does the most work: after-hours is when the calls happen and when the next listing looks best.
  • The busy greeting: honest capacity talk — "we're flat out this week, next booking is Thursday" — beats pretending otherwise and calling back with bad news. Callers respect a queue they can see.
  • The holiday/notice greeting: same bones as the closed one, plus the named reason and the exact reopen date. Pair it with the delayed opening notice so the door, the website, and the mailbox say the same thing.

If the business has a shared mailbox rather than a personal one, the script doesn't change — only Line 5 does. "Press zero" only works where someone is actually on the other end of the escape hatch; on a shared box, replace it with the after-hours number, said once, slowly.

Recording craft: making it sound like the shop

  • One take, standing up, in the room. Not the boardroom voice. Stand where you stand when you greet a customer and record there — room noise is authenticity, and callers can hear the difference between a person and a pitch.
  • Smile on Line 1. It is audible. The first sentence is the only one judged before it finishes.
  • Say the numbers slowly. The back time, the hours, the after-hours number — anything with digits gets a beat of space around it, because nobody rewinds a voicemail.
  • Name pronunciation check. If the business name gets mangled (it happens to every borrow-word and hyphenate), spell the alternative out: "Northside — that's the plumber on Bright Road, not the car yard." One sentence, saves ten wrong-number messages a week.
  • Re-record when anything changes. New hours, new location, new person answering — the greeting that says "back Monday" on a Wednesday is worse than no greeting, because the caller did the maths on false numbers.

The test call

Before the recording ships, ring the number from a phone that isn't yours and judge it as a caller with wet shoes and a slow afternoon would: Did I hear whose business this is? Do I know when they'll call back? Do I know what to say? Is the faster path actually faster — does the booking link go to a calendar and not a homepage? Does the urgent path answer? Then leave yourself a test message and check it lands where a named human reads it. The missed call text back setup and the greeting are the same machine's two outputs — the text covers the ring that never connected, the greeting covers the one that reached the beep — and both must land in the same inbox that someone actually opens.

The four traps

  • The full mailbox. The greeting that says "leave a message" on a box that hasn't been emptied is the most expensive sentence in small business — the caller stays for the beep, the beep declines them, and they never try again. Set a weekly reminder to empty the box; it is a thirty-second job with a first-job-sized return.
  • The comedy greeting. The joke recording, the singing dog, the "speak after the tone" bit. It gets a laugh once from a regular and loses every first-time caller, who now cannot tell if the business is real. Save the personality for the message you leave; keep the greeting factual and warm.
  • The greeting from the previous rebrand. Old name, old suburb, old extension number — every stale fact costs a little trust and occasionally the whole call. The monthly audit (below) catches it.
  • The mailbox nobody checks. A perfect greeting that promises a callback "before 2 pm" from a box last opened in March is a lie with good production values. The back time in Line 2 is a commitment the rota has to keep; if the check-in time changes, the recording changes with it.

The four-minute monthly audit

  1. Ring your own number. Every greeting currently live — workday, closed, holiday — takes its turn. Twenty seconds each.
  2. Fact-check every digit. Hours, reopen times, booking links, the after-hours number. Anything that has changed since the recording was made goes on the re-record list.
  3. Empty the box and count. How many messages, how old is the oldest, did every one get a callback? A box with a week-old unread message is a lead with a timestamp — call it today and apologise for the gap. The lead response time checklist has the speed rule that applies here like everywhere else.
  4. Listen once as a stranger. Would this recording make you stay on the line, or start scrolling? If the honest answer is the second one, the five lines above are ten minutes away.

Worked example: the barbershop's beep

The two-chair barbershop from the missed-call page fixed its lunchtime leak with text-back — and then found the beep was still eating calls. The old greeting was the carrier default: "the person you are calling is unavailable." Nothing wrong, nothing right. Callers hung up at the beep and rang the other shop, which answered.

The fix took one coffee break. New greeting, five lines: "You've reached Two Chairs Barbering — we're mid-haircut. We check this box every hour and will call you back before the end of the day. Leave your name, number, and the day you'd like. Or book straight in at twochairs.com/book — it goes to the same phone." No urgent line needed; nobody needs a haircut at 7 pm. The owner recorded it twice, kept the second take, and did the same ten-minute job for the after-hours recording that weekend.

The following fortnight the beep stopped leaking: message count went up (good — callers now stayed), and the booking link picked up the callers who never wanted a callback at all. Total spend: ten minutes, twice, and a reminder to empty the box every Friday.
