Google Business Profile Optimization Checklist for Small Businesses: The Free Storefront Most Owners Leave Half-Open

For a local business, the Google Business Profile is not a listing — it is the storefront. More first-time customers meet you there than on your website, your Instagram, and your front door combined: they search, they see the card — photos, hours, reviews, the button that calls you — and they decide in about thirty seconds. Most owners finish half of it on a Tuesday in year one and never come back: unverified, three photos from opening week, hours that went stale the first Christmas, and questions from strangers sitting unanswered at the top of the page. A half-open storefront reads exactly like a closed one. This is the checklist that finishes it — the one-time setup, the weekly ten-minute routine, and the traps that get profiles suspended.

Claim it, then lock it

An unclaimed profile is a storefront anyone can redecorate. Strangers "suggest an edit" that sticks, competitors add photos, and the phone number sometimes points somewhere that isn't you. Before optimizing anything:

  • Claim and verify. Search your exact business name on Google; if the card says Own this business?, you haven't. Verification is usually a postcard, phone, or video recording of your storefront — finish it, because an unverified profile can be suspended at any edit.
  • Hunt duplicates. Old addresses, a listing created by a marketing agency in 2019, a typo twin. Every duplicate splits your reviews in half. Request removal or merge; keep the one with the review history.
  • Add managers, never share the login. The owner account stays with the owner; managers get invites that can be revoked the week the social-media freelancer changes.
  • Service-area businesses: hide the address on purpose. A mobile groomer, a cleaner, an electrician with no walk-in storefront sets a service area and hides the home address. Doing both — showing the address and calling it service-area — is a suspension ticket.

The three fields that decide whether you exist

Google matches a search to your profile on roughly three fields. Get these wrong and the best photos in the world don't matter:

  • Business name: the real-world name, exactly. The name on the sign. Not "Jim's Plumbing — Best 24/7 Emergency Plumber in Town." Keyword-stuffed names are the fastest route to a suspension, and reviewers report them for sport.
  • Primary category: the search that pays, not the dream. The category field is the strongest ranking signal you control. A bakery that wants to be a wedding venue is still "Bakery" first — "Wedding venue" can be a secondary category. Pick the category that matches the words a paying customer types at the moment of need ("emergency plumber", "dog groomer", "med spa"), then add secondaries for the adjacent money.
  • Attributes: the filters people actually tap. Wheelchair accessible, free Wi-Fi, women-owned, curbside pickup, appointment required, online care. These are checkboxes in search filters — an un-checked attribute makes you invisible to the customers filtering for it.

Hours: the field that manufactures one-stars

The most expensive field on the whole profile is opening hours, because it lies at the worst moment. A customer drives twenty minutes to a door that says "Open" on Google and finds a dark shop — that's not a lost sale, that's a one-star that says "Google said you were open."

  • Special hours for every single holiday and every single change. Christmas, public holidays, the staff-training Tuesday, the week you close for repainting. The card shows a red "Holiday hours" flag only if you set them.
  • The "temporarily closed" flag is a fuse, not a switch — set it for the flood, the renovation, the maternity leave, and take it off the day you reopen. Half the closed-looking profiles in any town are open; the owner flipped the flag during the 2020 lockdown and never checked again.
  • Update the hours the week the hours change — not "when things settle." The reopening notice template pairs with this: the profile gets the hours first, the sign gets them second.

Photos: the freshness rule

Customers read photo recency the way they read a review count. Google shows the dates; a set of four photos from opening week signals a business that may no longer exist.

  • Monthly minimum, forever. One phone photo a month is the whole rule. The camera roll you already have is the content calendar.
  • The six that earn their place: the exterior (so people recognize the door), the interior, the product or the menu, the team at work, the thing that makes you different (the wood-fired oven, the van, the waiting room with toys), and one of whatever sells most.
  • A real phone photo beats a stock photo — and it beats the ones customers upload, which is the point: upload first, or the first images of your business are chosen by strangers.

Questions and answers: seed it before strangers do

Anyone can ask a question on your profile, and anyone can answer it — unless you get there first. The Q&A block ranks like a menu item, and the owner's answer outranks a stranger's guess.

  • Write the five questions your phone answers every week. "Do you take walk-ins?" "Is parking nearby?" "Do you do cats?" "What's the price range?" Ask them from a personal account, answer them from the owner account, in two sentences each.
  • Upvote your answers so they stay on top, and check monthly — new questions appear with no notification worth trusting.
  • Answer strangers' questions the same week. An unanswered "do you deliver?" with three crowd-sourced wrong answers is a storefront you let someone else write.

Posts: the weekly flyer with a one-week shelf life

A Google post is a small card — photo, two sentences, a link — that appears on your profile for about a week. It is the only part of the profile that behaves like social media, and it is free advertising square in the middle of search results.

  • One post a week, two sentences, one photo, one link. The special, the new stock, the holiday hours, the opening in the storm — the post is where the temporary-closure notice gets its Google audience too.
  • Offer posts carry their own button ("View offer", "Book") — use them for anything with a date on it.
  • The stale signal is real: a profile whose newest post is 400 days old reads as dormant next door to a competitor posting weekly. When in doubt, the last post should never be older than the last month.

Products, services, and the menu with prices

The product and service sections are the only parts of the profile where you set the shelves. Empty shelves read as a business with nothing to sell.

  • List the top ten, with prices. "From $X" is enough — a price range filters out the wrong customer before the phone call does it badly.
  • Restaurants: the menu tab is not optional — and the menu photos should be the same menu the till prints, not a PDF from 2021.
  • Service businesses: name services the way customers say them. "Full groom — small dog, from $70", not "Canine aesthetic package A".

The rails: booking, messaging, and the phone that rings a real place

  • Booking link straight to the calendar — not to a homepage where the customer must find the button. Every extra click is a customer booking the competitor's easier profile.
  • Messaging: only turn it on if someone owns it. The chat button with a three-day reply time is worse than no chat button; the rule is the same as for reviews — answered inside 24 hours, by a named human, or switched off.
  • The phone number rings a real place during the hours you printed. Test it quarterly, from a phone that isn't yours. The number that routes to a dead extension undoes everything else on the card.

The review loop

Reviews are the visible half of the profile — the stars show up in search before anything else does — and they run on two rails: the ask and the reply. The ask happens at the moment of delight, named platform, two taps from a counter QR. The reply happens inside 24 hours, written for the next fifty readers. The full system — the fact-check, the five-part reply anatomy, the make-good ladder, the fresh-review bench — lives in the negative review response playbook. For this checklist, the profile-side rule is one line: reply to every review, good or bad, and never let the newest one sit unanswered.

The four numbers in Insights that tell you what to fix

Performance data arrives monthly and most owners never open it. Four numbers, four jobs:

  • Calls — how many tapped the phone button. If calls are low but views are high, the hours or the photos are scaring people off; if both are low, the category or the name is hiding you from the search entirely.
  • Direction requests — the local demand map. People pressed "directions" from a real street near you; the months with spikes tell you when demand walks past your door (and whether your hours were open for it).
  • Website clicks — the weakest of the three buttons for local intent; most customers who want you call or navigate. A profile that lives on website clicks alone is usually missing the booking link.
  • Top search terms — the words people typed before finding you. If the words don't match your primary category, the category is wrong. This is the field that tells you what customers call you — which is frequently not what's on the sign.

The weekly ten-minute routine

  • Reply to every review since last week (24-hour rule for the fresh ones).
  • Post one update: photo, two sentences, one link.
  • Check special hours against the calendar for the next six weeks.
  • Answer any new Q&A questions; upvote your answers.
  • Glance at the four Insights numbers; write one line in the ops log.
  • Monthly: add one photo, re-read the top five photos with fresh eyes.
  • Quarterly: call your own phone number, click your own booking link, re-test the messaging button, re-check the attributes against competitors.

Traps

  • The keyword-stuffed name ("Jim's Plumbing — Cheap 24/7 Emergency Plumbers Near You") — the fastest suspension in the handbook, and the manual review it triggers reads your whole profile with suspicion.
  • Review gating — the "only happy customers please tap here" funnel. It violates every platform's policy, and one screenshot costs you the listing.
  • Bought reviews — the burst of five-star reviews from accounts in other cities is the most obvious pattern on the internet, and the profile outlives the vendor by exactly one audit.
  • The address game — listing a virtual office or a home address for a business that doesn't receive customers there. Google suspends for it, and the customers who show up at the door expect the door to open.
  • Set-and-forget — the profile finished in year one, perfect on day one, three years stale. Every stale field is a small door closed.
  • Posts as ads only — every post shouting a discount trains nobody; the posts that work are the ones a neighbour would forward: hours changes, new stock, the honest "we're closed today, back Thursday."

Worked example: the mobile dog groomer and the direction-request map

A one-van dog groomer, two years in, working off a personal Facebook page and a profile created by a friend "back when." The card had her home suburb listed as the address, three photos from the first month, and 23 reviews she'd never once replied to.

  • Week one — the setup pass: verified the listing; switched it to a service-area business (address hidden, four suburbs named); primary category "Pet groomer"; attributes set (women-owned, appointment required); booking link pointed at the calendar she already used; the five phone questions written into Q&A ("do you do cats? no — dogs only", "how long does a full groom take? about two hours", "do you need my gate unlocked? yes — here's how that works").
  • Week two — the content pass: six fresh photos (the van, the table, three before-and-afters, one of her with a very small, very dubious poodle); ten services listed with "from" prices; the first post announcing two Tuesday slots.
  • Month one — the Insights read: direction requests clustered in two suburbs she'd been treating as "too far to bother." She named them as service areas, and the post schedule gained a Tuesday slot aimed at them. Calls rose first; bookings followed a fortnight later.
  • The review loop: a card with the QR and the words "Google reviews keep the van on the road — two taps, thank you" went into every completed groom. Nine reviews in the first two months, each replied to within a day, the one two-star ("she was 20 minutes late") answered with the traffic-parking explanation and a $10 credit on the next visit — and the reviewer's own update raised it to four stars.
  • Six months in: the profile generates more new-client bookings than word of mouth did in year one, at zero media spend — the storefront was always there; it just had the lights off.
