Blameless post-incident review template: find causes, not culprits

Every small team runs the same review the first time: someone asks "who pushed this?", the room goes quiet, the person who did it learns to hide the next one, and the incident repeats in six weeks wearing a different costume. The blame reflex feels like accountability and works like a leak — it teaches everyone that the safe move is silence. A blameless review is not softer accountability; it is stronger: it separates the person from the system and interrogates the system, because people do not cause outages — paths that let one keystroke reach production do. The template below fits 45 minutes, produces at most five actions with owners and dates, and is safe to run the week after your first real incident.

This is the template: the 72-hour clock, the participant list (including one outsider), the timeline table with detection latency, the four review questions, contributing factors instead of a single root cause, the five-action cap, the five blame traps with rewrites, the ground rules, distribution, and the three metrics that keep the practice honest.

1. The 72-hour clock

Schedule the review within 72 hours of resolution — sooner and the responders are still fried, later and the timeline is archaeology. Forty-five minutes, hard stop. If it needs longer, you are designing the fix in the meeting; stop and take the actions to owners.

2. Who is in the room

  • Facilitator — the person whose job in the meeting is to run the clock and enforce the ground rules. Not the most senior person; the person most likely to be talked over is the one the facilitator protects.
  • All responders — everyone who touched the incident, even for ten minutes. The ten-minute person often saw the turning point.
  • One outsider — someone who was not involved. Their job is to ask "why was that normal?" about things the insiders have stopped noticing.
  • Scribe — writes the timeline and actions live, shared screen. The writeup is mostly done before the meeting ends.
  • Nobody's manager as interrogator — managers attend, listen, and own actions like everyone else. A review where the boss asks the questions produces polished answers, not true ones.

4. The four questions

  • What happened? — in plain language, no jargon, as if explaining to a new hire's first week.
  • Why did it happen? — keep asking until you hit a system property, not a person. "Sam typo'd the config" is not a cause; "a single keystroke in a config file could reach production without a second pair of eyes" is.
  • What caught it, and how late? — name the detection that worked and the detection that didn't. This is where the cheap fixes live.
  • What would have made it smaller? — not "prevented", smaller. Smaller is achievable this month; prevented is a wish.

5. Contributing factors, not root cause

The single-root-cause hunt is blame with a spreadsheet. Real incidents are layered: a config change and a missing staging guard and an alert that fires too late and an on-call rotation with one person carrying four systems. Write contributing factors as a list; if the list has fewer than three entries, the room has stopped early. Each factor gets its own candidate action, and the cheapest one usually wins funding — a staging guard beats a rewrite.

6. The five-action cap

Reviews that end with fifteen actions end with zero shipped. Cap actions at five, each with an owner, a date inside two weeks, and a "smallest version that ships" definition. Mark any action that is a document change (runbook, checklist, alert text) as a same-week ship — documentation is the fastest durability you can buy.

7. The five blame traps (and their rewrites)

  • The name — "Sam broke it." → "The deploy path allowed one keystroke to reach production." Actions aim at the path.
  • The adjective — "a careless mistake." → "A mistake the system permitted to be invisible until checkout broke." Careless/careful is not a cause; it is a mood.
  • The counterfactual trial — "if you'd just checked…" → "What makes the check the default instead of the discipline?" Discipline does not scale; defaults do.
  • The hero story — "at least Dana stayed up all night." → "What made a midnight hero the plan?" Heroics are load-bearing evidence of a staffing or automation gap.
  • The review-as-verdict — "so who owns this mess?" → "Who owns the action?" Ownership attaches to forward work, never to punishment.

8. Ground rules read aloud at minute zero

  • We assume everyone did something reasonable with the information they had at the time.
  • Nothing said here appears in anyone's performance review — and that is enforced, not aspirational.
  • Anyone can say "I don't know" without penalty; unknowns go on the timeline as questions.
  • We critique systems and decisions, never names; the facilitator rewrites on the spot.

If your team cannot honestly hold rule two, do not run the review yet — a blameless theater that feeds a PIP at year end is worse than no review, because it burns the trust the next real review needs.

9. Distribution: where the writeup goes

A review nobody reads is a meeting with extra steps. Three destinations, one page each: a summary post in the team channel (timeline graphic, three factors, five actions), the full writeup in the incident log next to the severity call, and a one-line entry in the weekly ops digest. Past reviews are training data for new hires — a new engineer who reads the last six writeups inherits your scar tissue without paying for it.

10. Metrics that keep it honest

  • Action completion at 30 days — target 80%+. A review whose actions die is teaching everyone that reviews are theater.
  • Repeat-incident rate — same contributing factor within 90 days means the action treated a symptom.
  • Detection latency trend — minutes from user impact to page. If this number is not falling across reviews, your actions are all guards and no sensors.

Worked example: eight-person fintech, the Friday deploy

A config typo in a Friday 17:40 deploy broke checkout for 61 minutes. The first draft review (not run) was going to be short: "Sam, who merged on a Friday, owns this." The blameless version found four contributing factors instead: no staging guard on config changes, an alert threshold that waited for fifteen minutes of errors, a change-freeze calendar that existed but excluded "config-only" changes, and a review culture where skipping a second pair of eyes was the norm at 17:30. Three actions shipped that month — the staging smoke test, the tightened alert, and the freeze calendar amended so config-only deploys count as changes. Six months later the repeat rate on config-caused incidents was zero, and nobody had needed a single remediation conversation about a person.
