Content Calendar Template for Solo Creators: Plan a Month in One Sitting

Solo creators don't fail at content from lack of ideas; they fail at the daily "what do I post" decision, which drains the exact energy that production needs. A content calendar moves that decision to once a month. Here's a template structure you can fill in a single sitting, plus the repurposing loop that makes a month of content out of a handful of real pieces.

The template: columns that earn their place

Keep the calendar to six columns. Date — publication slot. Channel — where it goes. Pillar — which of your three to four content themes it belongs to (this column is what keeps a calendar from becoming a random assortment). Format — essay, checklist, short video, screenshot thread. Asset — link to the draft. Status — idea / drafted / ready / published. Anything beyond these six becomes maintenance overhead that a solo creator abandons by month two.

The one-sitting monthly planning session

Step 1 (10 min): list this month's three to four pillars — the topics you want to be known for. Step 2 (15 min): brainstorm under each pillar; rough titles only. Step 3 (10 min): assign dates and channels, respecting your realistic cadence — three quality pieces a week beats seven aspirational ones that collapse in week two. Step 4: mark which pieces are original (new thinking, takes real work — usually one or two a week) versus derived (repurposed or curated, fast). Step 5 (15 min): batch-create the derived ones immediately, while the context is loaded.

The repurposing loop that does the heavy lifting

Every original piece should produce at least three derived items: a short-form version (key takeaway as a post), a visual (screenshot, mockup, or diagram), and a newsletter blurb. Derived content isn't filler — it's how a piece reaches the part of your audience that missed the original. Practical ratio: for every hour of original production, expect 15 minutes to split it into derivatives, and schedule those on non-overlapping days. A month with eight originals becomes a calendar of twenty-plus slots, all traceable to real work.

Keeping it honest

Two maintenance rules keep the calendar useful: review it every weekly review (drop or shuffle what didn't happen — a calendar that lies to you gets ignored), and track which pillar actually drives replies and traffic monthly, then adjust pillar weight toward evidence instead of habit. If every week ends with three unfilled slots, your cadence is fiction; cut it to what you actually shipped for two straight weeks, then grow.

Honest limits

A calendar schedules work; it doesn't create insight. If every slot is filled with derivative content, output stays consistent and forgettable — protect the original pieces first and let the calendar organize them. Expect the plan to drift mid-month, especially around launches; the calendar's job is to make the drift visible in minutes, not to prevent it. And don't tool-hop: a shared doc or spreadsheet you actually open beats a system you abandon in three weeks.
