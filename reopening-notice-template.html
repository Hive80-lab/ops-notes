Reopening notice for small businesses — the verified-reopen gate, the channel flip, and the make-good ledger

A reopening notice is two documents, same as the closure notice it answers: a customer-facing announcement and an operational record (what was verified, at what time, by whom — because the reopen you announce must already be true). The first one wins back the revenue the closure cost you; the second one is what protects you when a landlord, an insurer, or a customer asks “you said you were open — why was the door locked?” The closure notice (temporary closure notice) promised a reopen date and a confirm-the-reopen line. This page is how you keep that promise: the seven-check gate that runs before anything is announced, the channel flip in reverse with the one channel that moves first, the seven lines every reopen notice contains, the make-good ledger for the customers you still owe, and the three phases of messaging between “we're open” and “back to normal.” — the same gate applies to a weather-day reopen, where the lot and the walkway are the gate; the night-before discipline that makes it possible is in the weather-day closure checklist

1. The gate: seven checks before a single announcement

The rule that prevents every reopen disaster: the notice goes out after the gate passes, not before. An announcement is a commitment; announcing “open tomorrow 7am” and opening at 9:30 converts a closure problem into a trust problem. Run these seven checks in order — fifteen minutes, one person, written down:

  1. People and stock. The roster confirmed for reopen day, and the physical inventory that lets you serve (ingredients, parts, stock, access cards, keys). If one supplier delivery is still outstanding, note it as a limitation for line 3 below — not a reason to delay.
  2. The thing that closed you is actually fixed. The pipe patched and pressure-tested, the power stable, the system patched and tested. “The contractor says it's done” is not a check; running water at full pressure is. Photograph or log the verification — this is the operational record.
  3. One customer journey, end to end, done by you. Walk in as a customer: call the number, place the order, pay by card, take delivery. The payment terminal is the thing that fails at half of all reopenings — it sat unused for days and nobody tested it.
  4. The backlog triaged. Every order, booking, message, and delivery that piled up during the closure is listed and assigned: honored as-is, rescheduled with a date, refunded, or cancelled with the make-good. The reopen notice says what happens to the backlog — so you need to know before you write it.
  5. Channels restored. Voicemail back to the standard greeting, autoresponder off or rewritten, the website banner ready to come down, the pinned closure post ready to be replaced. A half-restored channel (autoresponder still saying “closed until Thursday” on Thursday afternoon) tells customers the right hand doesn't know what the left is doing.
  6. Google Business Profile hours correct for reopen day — checked the evening before. Maps is the channel customers check before they drive; a CLOSED pin on an unlocked door sends them to the competitor and generates the reviews that follow you for months.
  7. The make-good ledger exists. One row per customer you owe something (see §4). The reopen notice commits to making these good — the ledger is what makes that sentence true.

Time-stamp the result: “Reopen gate passed 05:45 Thu — seven of seven checks, verified by [initials].” If one check fails, open in a limited state and say so (line 3 of the notice) rather than delaying the whole reopen for one line item.

2. The channel flip: the closure list in reverse, one channel moved to the front

The closure notice ran its channels in discovery order (door sign, website banner, Google hours, voicemail, autoresponder, social, scheduled customers). Reopening runs the same list with reversed emphasis — because on reopen day the channels aren't informing anyone, they're correcting the stale information the closure left behind:

  1. Google Business Profile hours — first, before the doors open. On closure day this was the most-forgotten channel; on reopen day it's the one that must flip earliest, because Maps drives the arrival. Flip special hours back to normal the evening before, verify at open, and take down any closure post or photo you added to the profile.
  2. The door sign. Down, or replaced with a clean reopen line. A taped-over closure sign with today's date scribbled in pen reads as improvisation; a proper sign reads as control.
  3. Voicemail greeting. Restore the standard recording. A voicemail still reciting the closure is the second thing (after Maps) that tells a caller the business doesn't know it's open.
  4. Email autoresponder. Off, or rewritten to the reopen text. Then read the inbox it guarded — the backlog it collected is the raw material of your make-good ledger.
  5. Website banner and homepage line. Replace the closure banner with one reopen line rather than deleting it silently — customers arriving mid-week after a short closure may never have seen the closure notice at all.
  6. Social: replace the pinned post. Closure pin down the moment the reopen post goes up. One pinned post at all times — a profile still pinning last week's disaster reads as a business still in it.
  7. Scheduled customers, individually. Every booking, appointment, delivery, or pickup that was cancelled or moved gets a personal confirmation of its new slot — their name and their time in it, not a broadcast. This is the highest-value hour of the entire reopen.

3. The seven lines every reopening notice contains

  1. What is open, and from exactly when. “The Elm Street location reopens Thursday 7am.” Same specificity as the closure notice — “we're back!” without a when forces everyone to ask.
  2. Hours — including anything temporary. Normal hours, or the temporary ones plus the date regular hours resume. State the hours that are true on day one, not the ones you hope for by week two.
  3. What is still limited, and until when. Half the menu, online orders shipping from the other site, the back room still out of service. Stating a limitation costs almost nothing; a customer discovering one costs you the review. If everything is genuinely full-service, say that plainly — “no limitations, full service from open” — so the absence of the line reads as a decision, not an oversight.
  4. What changed while you were closed. The fix, in one sentence a customer cares about: new kitchen plumbing, replaced card terminals, the booking system that kept failing rebuilt. This line converts the closure from a liability into evidence you maintain the place.
  5. The make-good line. “Everyone whose booking was moved has been contacted with their new time; anyone we missed, reply to this email and we'll sort it the same day.” One sentence, specific, with the deadline on your side.
  6. The contact channel that will be answered. Same rule as closure: one channel, named owner, stated response time. Reopen week generates more reply-to traffic than the closure did.
  7. The thank-you — short and real. One line to the staff and customers who carried you through. Not a paragraph; sincerity scales inversely with length.

4. The make-good ledger: the customers you still owe

A closure breaks promises — bookings missed, orders late, prepaid things undelivered. The reopen is when those debts are settled or defaulted on, and the difference is whether they live in a ledger or in memory. The ledger is one table, started during the closure, finished within a week of reopening:

Who · what was promised · the new promise (date-bound) · delivered (date).

  • Honor the original price. A prepaid service delivered after reopening at the old price, even if prices changed in between, is the cheapest goodwill you will ever buy — and the fastest route to the review that names you.
  • Upgrade, don't discount. The make-good for a missed booking is the next slot plus something (priority pick of times, a small add-on, first in the door), not a percentage off. Discounts reprice the relationship; upgrades reward it.
  • Date-bound or it isn't a promise. “We'll make it up to you” with no date is an apology. “Your replacement slot is Thursday 2pm, confirmed” is a make-good. Every row carries a date both sides could recite.
  • Close the loop in writing. When a row is done, one line to the customer: “Done — and thanks for your patience through the closure.” Closed rows are your proof, at the next wobble, that you finish what you start.

Target: every row date-bound within 24 hours of reopening, every row closed within seven days. A reopened business with an open make-good ledger is one bad Monday away from losing the customers the closure didn't.

5. The three phases of “back” — don't spend them all on day one

The commonest reopen mistake is treating “we're open” as a single announcement instead of a three-phase message arc:

  1. Phase one — open (day one): the seven lines above, everywhere the closure notice appeared. Capacity honesty: state the limitations, accept the smaller day. This phase's only job is to make “open” true everywhere a customer might look.
  2. Phase two — settled (first week): the make-good ledger closing row by row, plus one short update (“full menu back from Thursday”) if phase one carried limitations. This phase's job is to make “open” complete.
  3. Phase three — normal (weeks two–four): normal operations, no more closure references in every post — but keep the record. The closure-and-reopen file (gate results, ledger, timeline) is the story you'll be glad to have when a landlord, insurer, or licensor asks — and the case study you'll write if you ever sell.

Businesses that skip phase two look restored but keep bleeding goodwill one forgotten booking at a time. Businesses that stall in phase two — the closure in every post for months — convert a bad week into a permanent identity. The arc has an end; walk it.

6. Worked example — the café from the closure notice, reopened

The same three-day closure from the temporary closure notice page: a 9-seat café closed Tuesday–Thursday after a burst pipe flooded the kitchen. The reopen, hour by hour:

  • Wed 17:30 (evening before): plumber signs off; pressure test logged with a photo. Gate checks 1–3 done early: roster confirmed, kitchen restocked, card terminal test charge run — theirs had dropped into offline mode during the closure; caught, reset, verified with a $1 charge refunded on the spot. This is the failure that half of reopenings discover at the register, in front of a queue.
  • Wed 18:00: Google hours flipped back to normal and verified; Thursday's special hours deleted; closure photo taken off the profile. Voicemail and autoresponder rewritten. Website banner replaced with one line: “We're back — regular hours from Thursday 7am.”
  • Wed 18:20: backlog triage: 41 window bookings from the closure window rescheduled into next week's gaps, 2 preorders refunded in full plus a free-coffee code, 3 catering quotes answered. Make-good ledger opened: 46 rows, every one date-bound.
  • Thu 05:45: reopen gate formally passed — seven of seven checks, initialed by the owner. Door sign replaced; social pin swapped (closure post down, reopen post up).
  • Thu 06:00–09:00: doors open at 6am as promised. 41 rescheduled bookings confirmed individually by 9:00 — name, new time, first coffee on the house.
  • Thu 16:00: 44 of 46 ledger rows closed (a confirmed rescheduled slot counts as closed); the 2 catering jobs delivered Friday. Ledger empty by Monday.
  • The outcome that matters: the first Google review after reopening, posted Friday: “Flooded kitchen Tuesday, back open Thursday, they called me about my booking before I even thought to ask. This is how you run a place.” The closure became evidence of competence — because the reopen was verified before it was announced, and the promises had a ledger.

7. The five traps

  1. Announcing before the gate passes. The reopen notice written on hope converts one bad week into two: the closure, then the “you said you'd be open” follow-up. The gate costs fifteen minutes; the broken promise costs reviews.
  2. Flipping the banner but not Google hours. The banner is the channel you check; Maps is the channel they check. A CLOSED pin on an open door on reopen morning undoes the whole notice for exactly the customers most ready to return.
  3. “We're back!” with no hours, no limitations, no make-good. An announcement that forces every reader to ask a follow-up question is one that half of them won't act on. The seven lines exist so nobody has to ask.
  4. Treating rescheduled customers as “they'll figure it out.” The person whose Tuesday booking evaporated doesn't know their new slot exists until someone tells them, individually, with the time in it. A broadcast is not a confirmation.
  5. Reopening without saying what changed. If the pipe, the terminal, or the system was fixed, say so in one sentence. “We're back” after a silence reads as “nothing was wrong, we were just closed”; “we're back, and the kitchen has new plumbing” reads as a business that maintains itself. The fix story is free; leaving it out is the only waste on this page.

8. Metrics — the reopen scorecard

  • Gate-pass-to-announcement lag: the notice should go out within hours of the gate passing; a multi-day lag means the comms stalled while the ops were fine.
  • Google-hours flip time: hours correct before the first open minute. Track it as a hard gate, not an average.
  • Scheduled-customer confirmation rate within 24h: every cancelled booking, appointment, or delivery acknowledged with its new slot inside a day. Target 100% — it's a bounded list, not an aspiration.
  • Make-good ledger closure rate at 7 days: rows closed / rows opened. Target 100%; rows still open at day 7 get escalated, not explained.
  • Review mentions of the closure/reopen: the qualitative metric — how returning customers narrate your handling. Three words in a review (“they called me”) are worth more than every internal number on this list.

9. Copy-paste one-page reopening template

Door sign, homepage line, email, and pinned post can all be the same block. Fill the brackets, delete the lines that don't apply, and check it against the seven lines:

WE'RE BACK — [Location] reopened [Day], [Time].

[Regular hours: ___ — or “Temporary hours until [date]: ___”]
[What's still limited, until when — or: “No limitations — full service from open”]
[One sentence on what changed: “The kitchen has new plumbing / the card system is replaced / ___”]
[Make-good: “Everyone whose booking was moved has been contacted with their new time. If we missed you, email or call [channel] — we'll sort it the same day.”]
[Questions: [channel], answered within [time] by [name/role]]
Thank you for waiting on us — see you this week.

And the operational-record line, kept for the file rather than the window: “Reopen gate passed [day/time] — seven of seven checks, verified by [initials]. Make-good ledger opened [date] with [N] rows; all rows date-bound within 24h; ledger closed [date].”
