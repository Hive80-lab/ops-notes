Temporary closure notice for small businesses — say it once, say it right, reopen clean

A closure is two documents at once: a customer-facing notice (what's closed, until when, what still works, how you'll confirm the reopen) and an operational record (who decided, who was told, when, through which channel). The first one protects revenue and goodwill; the second one is what you'll be asked for by the landlord, the insurer, the licensor, and the next person who inherits the mess. This page gives you the one-page notice template, the channel order so you don't forget the one channel customers actually check, duration tiers so a 6-hour power cut doesn't get a 2-week treatment, and the verified-reopen checklist so you never announce a reopen that isn't real yet.

1. The seven lines every closure notice contains

  1. What is closed. The site, the door, the specific service — not "we" in the abstract. "The Elm Street location" if you have two and only one is dark.
  2. Dates and times, both ends. Closed from 2pm Tue, planned reopen 7am Fri. A planned reopen you can move is worth ten times an "until further notice." If the reopen date genuinely can't be estimated, give the date of the next update instead — "we'll confirm by Thursday 4pm either way."
  3. The reason, in one honest sentence. "Burst pipe in the kitchen" beats "unforeseen circumstances." Honesty here is free goodwill; vagueness reads as either disaster or something you're hiding.
  4. What still works. Online orders shipping from the other site? Email answered? Gift cards honored? State it — half of lost closure revenue is customers who assumed everything stopped.
  5. The contact channel that WILL be answered. One address or number, named owner, stated response time ("we'll reply within a day"). Never point people at a channel nobody is watching.
  6. The make-good for scheduled customers. Every appointment, booking, delivery, or pickup in the closure window gets contacted individually — this line commits you to it. Outage email templates →
  7. How you'll confirm the reopen. "We'll post the reopening here and on Google the moment we're back." This sentence is why the reopen notice gets read.

2. The channel order (the one most businesses forget is #3)

The order matters because it follows how customers actually discover you're closed: they're standing at the door, or they checked Google Maps, or they called.

  1. The door / window sign. Printed, dated, with the reopen date and the contact line. Ten minutes. This is for the person already in the car park.
  2. Website banner + a homepage line. Same text as the door sign. If the closure will outlast the page's top card, swap the homepage hero line — a banner people have banner-blindness for is not a notice.
  3. Google Business Profile hours. Mark the special hours closed for the window, and fix them the day you reopen. Customers check Maps before they drive. An OPEN pin on a locked door generates the angriest reviews you'll ever read — and it's the single most-forgotten channel on this list.
  4. Voicemail greeting. New recording, same seven lines. The greeting is often the first human-sounding thing a customer meets.
  5. Email autoresponder. For the inbox that orders and bookings flow through. Same text, plus the reopen-confirm promise.
  6. Social: one pinned post. Post once, pin it, stop. Ten closure-day posts read as chaos; one pinned post reads as control.
  7. Scheduled customers, individually. Every booking, appointment, delivery, or recurring pickup inside the window — personal message with the make-good. This is the difference between "they closed" and "they called me."

3. Duration tiers — match the response to the hours, not the drama

  • Under 24 hours (power cut, burst pipe, staff emergency): door sign, voicemail, Google hours, individual calls to anyone scheduled in the window. Skip the website banner if the site drives no walk-ins.
  • If the under-24h case is a “maybe” rather than a “no”, that's a delayed opening, not a closure — same channels, plus a named update time; see the delayed opening notice template.
  • 1–7 days (repair, deep clean, inspection, flood): the full seven-line notice on every channel, autoresponder on, pinned post, and the landlord/insurer/licensor notification if the lease or policy requires it (many require notice within 24–48h of any closure — check your lease before the closure, in peacetime). Business continuity plan →
  • Over 7 days (fire, rebuild, relocation): everything above, plus: a weekly update cadence stated in the notice, a reopen-date estimate with a named next-update date, staff scheduling sorted in writing, and suppliers paused or redirected in writing so stock doesn't arrive at a dark store.

4. What NOT to do (the five closure traps)

  • "Until further notice" with no date of any kind. Customers read it as either permanent or evasive. A planned-reopen date you later move costs you nothing; a vacuum costs you the customer.
  • Silent voicemail. The default carrier greeting says "the person you called is unavailable" while the door is locked. The mismatch reads as sloppiness, and it's where cancellations breed.
  • Maps says OPEN while the door is locked. The angriest one-star reviews are almost always "drove 30 minutes, closed, Google said open." Two minutes in the GBP dashboard prevents all of them.
  • Announcing the reopen before it's verified. The reopen notice that's followed by a same-day "sorry, still closed" post is the second incident. Verify first, then announce (see §5).
  • Letting scheduled customers find out from the sign. The booking for Thursday 2pm gets a personal message, not a drive past a dark storefront. One hundred percent contact rate is the metric.

5. The verified-reopen checklist (run it BEFORE the reopen post)

  1. Utilities and environment actually on and holding. Power, water, HVAC, refrigeration back in band — not "switched on" but holding (fridge back under 5°C, not "the compressor hums").
  2. Safety-critical equipment tested. POS transaction goes end-to-end, alarm arms, fire exits clear, the thing that failed is the thing that's fixed — name it on the checklist.
  3. Staff confirmed for the first shift. By name, not "everyone should be back."
  4. Stock and supplies checked. What spoiled, what expired, what needs an emergency order before the doors open.
  5. Contact channels reverted. Voicemail back to the normal greeting, autoresponder off, website banner down, Google hours corrected before the reopen post goes up.
  6. The make-good ready. Credits, discounts, or first-week offer prepared so the reopening message can carry it.

Then the reopening notice: lead with the verified reopen, thank customers for the patience, state what changed for the better (the fix, the upgrade), make the make-good offer, and keep the closure notice visible for 48 hours with a one-line redirect ("we reopened Friday — details in the post below").

6. Worked example — 14-seat café, 3-day closure after a burst pipe

Tuesday 9:12am: the pipe above the kitchen lets go. Staff kill the water main by 9:20. The owner prints the door sign from the template — closed from 2pm Tuesday, planned reopen Friday 7am, "burst pipe in the kitchen, repairs booked," orders-and-pickup still live through the website, hello@ answered within a day, every booking in the window to be contacted with a make-good, reopen confirmed on Google and here the moment it's real. Door sign up 9:40.

By 10:15 Google Business Profile hours are marked closed through Thursday; 10:30 the voicemail is re-recorded; 10:40 the autoresponder is on; 10:50 one pinned social post. 11:00–13:00 the bookings list from the till is worked top to bottom: 62 bookings in the window, all 62 contacted individually — 41 moved to the following week, 17 taken as credits, 4 cancelled. The landlord is emailed at 11:15 (the lease wanted notice "promptly"; the insurer's line is logged with a time stamp). The repairs invoice, the plumber's report, and the closure notice itself go in one folder — which is what the business-interruption claim later asks for.

Friday 6:40am the verified-reopen checklist runs: fridge at 3.8°C holding, POS transaction gone end-to-end, alarm armed and disarmed, the nine staff for opening shift confirmed by name, one emergency produce order placed Thursday, voicemail/autoresponder/banner/Google hours all reverted before the 8:05am reopening post goes up with the "first coffee's on us this morning" make-good. Four of the 17 no-shows from closure days redeem credits that weekend. The one metric the owner now tracks monthly: door-sign-to-all-channels was 1h53m that Tuesday — the target is under 60 minutes, and the thing that ate the margin was the Google hours update nobody remembered until a customer called about it.

7. The metrics (four numbers, reviewed after every closure)

  • Time-to-full-notice: door sign up → last channel updated. Target: under 60 minutes.
  • Scheduled-customer contact rate: bookings in window contacted ÷ bookings in window. Target: 100%, every time.
  • Verify-before-announce: reopen checklist completed → reopening post published. Target: 100% of reopens verified first, zero retraction posts.
  • Day-1 channel accuracy: voicemail, Google hours, and website banner each say the same thing on day 1. Target: 3 of 3 (one stale channel is the classic half-closure).

8. The one-page template (copy, fill, post)

TEMPORARY CLOSURE — [BUSINESS / LOCATION NAME]

Closed from: [day, date, time]
Planned reopen: [day, date, time]  (if unsure: "next update by [day, time]")
Why (one honest sentence): [reason]

What still works: [online orders / email / gift cards / other site]
Reach us at: [one named channel] — we reply within [stated window]

Every booking or delivery in this window will be contacted personally.
We'll confirm the reopen right here and on Google the moment it's real.

— [Name], [role] · [date posted]

Keep the filled copy in the closure folder with the landlord/insurer notifications and the repair invoices — that folder is your business-interruption evidence and your next closure's head start.
